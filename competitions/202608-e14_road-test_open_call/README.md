## Rules

### RoadTest Open Call

Has there ever been a product that you wanted to test and review but it has not
been featured in another RoadTest here on the community? Now is your chance!

Submit an application to review any product of your choosing under $175 (USD)
on the Newark / Farnell / element14 online stores.

The ten best applicants will receive their requested product for FREE and get
to keep it in return for writing a review.

### How To Apply

Click the button on the right to enroll by putting in your application. Here is
an idea of what to include:

- The product you want to review and why you have chosen it
- The details of the test plan or review you plan on carrying out
- A brief description of your background, general experience level, and
  familiarity with the technology (embedded systems / microcontrollers /
  programming / prototyping etc.)

A more innovative and/or thorough review of a new or overlooked product is more
likely to give you a chance of being selected, as long as the ambition of your
review matches up with your experience level and familiarity with the
technology.

### Example Product Review Possibilities

So, what kind of product could you review?

- A single board computer (eg. Arduino Uno Q)
- An all-in-one kit (eg. development kit, motor control kit, optical, wireless
  transceiver kit)
- Bench equipment (eg. power supply, oscilloscope, soldering station)

Once you know the product you want to receive and review, what sort of review
could you write?

The only limit is your imagination but here are a few examples:

- The out-of-the-box experience of a product - Do an unboxing, power the
  product up, check any pre-programmed options or features, and follow the user
  guide.
- Performance test - Measure accuracy and see if the product is faster/slower
  than specifications
- Cybersecurity evaluation - Test for security holes and vulnerabilities
- Test a product to destruction! - Show how a product holds up in hazardous
  conditions such as high temperatures or rugged environments.

# StereoStrike — Road Test Test Plan & Procedure

## 2× Raspberry Pi Global Shutter Camera (SC0926, Sony IMX296)

**Project:** Hardware-Synchronized Stereo Vision for Ballistic Trajectory
Prediction

| | |
|---|---|
| **Product Under Test** | 2× Raspberry Pi Global Shutter Camera (SC0926 — Sony IMX296 sensor, C/CS-mount) |
| **Test Duration** | 8–10 weeks from receipt of hardware |
| **Primary Skills** | Embedded Linux, Python/OpenCV, hardware modification (SMD soldering), computer vision |
| **Personal Motivation** | Groundwork for a larger visual-trajectory-prediction project (ball-cannon/gimbal interception) planned on an Arduino VENTUNO Q later this year — this road test validates the stereo camera pipeline on hardware already owned (Raspberry Pi CM5 dev kit, from a separate road test) before committing it to the bigger build |

## 1. Project Overview

StereoStrike investigates whether the Raspberry Pi Global Shutter Camera can be
turned into a genuine hardware-synchronized stereo pair using its documented but
rarely-tested external trigger (XTR) pin — and whether the resulting rig is
accurate enough to predict the flight path of a moving object in real time.

Individually, the SC0926 is well covered on the community: its global shutter
and fast-motion suitability are well known. What isn't well covered is the
**multi-camera synchronization story** — Raspberry Pi's own documentation
describes triggering the camera externally via the XTR pin, and states that
multiple cameras wired to the same pulse can be synchronised, but there is very
little published evidence of anyone actually doing this, characterising the
accuracy, or documenting the hardware modification required. This road test
closes that gap.

The review will produce a fully documented hardware modification guide, a
working synchronized-capture pipeline, and measured stereo depth accuracy —
using a real-world test case (predicting where a thrown or kicked ball will
land) rather than a synthetic bench target.

## 2. Problem Statement

### 2.1 The Documentation Gap

Raspberry Pi's own camera documentation states the GS camera can be triggered
externally by pulsing the XTR connection, and that multiple cameras can share
the same pulse for synchronisation — but achieving this requires a physical
board modification (removing a resistor, soldering a wire to an SMD touchpoint)
that isn't covered in any official walkthrough with photos, and community forum
threads report real friction (frame-count mismatches, kernel trigger-mode
parameter issues) getting it working reliably.

### 2.2 The Applied Test Case

Rather than validate sync in the abstract, this review uses genuine outdoor
ballistic motion as the test signal — throwing/kicking a ball across a measured
distance (a neighbouring pitch-sized open space) and using the synced stereo
pair to estimate its 3D trajectory and predicted landing point. This produces a
concrete, checkable accuracy number (predicted vs. actual landing position)
rather than a qualitative "it worked" result, and doubles as direct groundwork
for a planned interception-robot project on a separate SBC.

### 3.3 System Architecture Summary

Both SC0926 cameras connect to the CM5 IO board's two independent MIPI CSI
ports, each having first been modified to expose the XTR external-trigger input
by removing resistor R11 and soldering a wire to the XTR touchpoint. A shared
PWM trigger signal — generated from a CM5 GPIO pin through a resistor divider to
bring the logic level down to the camera's expected voltage — is wired to both
cameras' XTR lines simultaneously, so a single pulse train drives both shutters
in lockstep. Captured frame pairs are timestamped, stored, and processed through
an OpenCV stereo-calibration and triangulation pipeline running on the CM5 CPU.

## 4. Vision & Trajectory Pipeline

### 4.1 Pipeline Stages

| Stage | Method | Task | Running On |
|---|---|---|---|
| Detection | Colour/blob or lightweight trained detector | Locate ball centroid per frame, per camera | CM5 CPU |
| Stereo correspondence | OpenCV calibration + disparity | Convert matched 2D detections to 3D position | CM5 CPU |
| Trajectory fit | Closed-form parabolic fit (ballistic) | Fit `y = ½gt² + v₀t` from position samples | CM5 CPU |
| Landing prediction | Extrapolation from fitted trajectory | Predict landing coordinates and time-to-land | CM5 CPU |

### 4.2 Synchronization Verification Logic

Before any trajectory work begins, sync accuracy itself must be established as a
measured quantity, not assumed from the wiring diagram:

- Both cameras capture a fast-moving reference object (dropped ball)
  simultaneously
- Frame timestamps and frame counts are compared pair-by-pair to confirm
  lockstep capture with no dropped or duplicated frames on either side
- Any residual timing offset is measured directly by comparing the reference
  object's position across the pair at a known drop speed, and reported as a
  quantified sync error (milliseconds), not a pass/fail claim

### 4.3 Trajectory Accuracy Logic

- A minimum of 4–6 well-spaced 3D position samples are required before a
  trajectory fit is attempted
- Predicted landing coordinates are compared against a measured, marked
  ground-truth landing point
- Accuracy is reported as landing-position error (cm) and time-to-land error
  (ms), across varied throw speed, angle, and distance

## 5. Test Procedure

### Week 1: Hardware Setup & Baseline

**Phase 1A: Single-Camera Bring-Up**

1. Connect one SC0926 to the CM5 IO board via libcamera, confirm detection and
   image capture
2. Repeat for the second unit on the other CSI port, confirm both function
   independently
3. Fit the 16mm telephoto lens to each, confirm focus/back-focus at the intended
   working distance
4. Benchmark baseline single-camera frame rate and exposure range as shipped
   (before any modification)

**Phase 1B: XTR Hardware Modification**

1. Identify R11 and the XTR touchpoint on each camera board per Raspberry Pi's
   documented modification
2. Remove R11 on both units, solder a wire lead to each XTR touchpoint
3. Visually inspect and continuity-test each modification before power-up
4. Confirm each camera still captures correctly in normal (non-triggered) mode
   post-modification, ruling out accidental damage

### Week 2: Trigger Circuit & Synchronized Capture

**Phase 2A: Trigger Circuit Build**

1. Build a resistor-divider circuit from a CM5 GPIO pin to bring the trigger
   pulse to the camera's expected logic level
2. Wire both cameras' XTR lines to the same trigger source
3. Write a PWM trigger script, confirm pulse frequency directly controls
   achieved frame rate on both cameras

**Phase 2B: Sync Verification**

1. Capture a dropped-ball reference sequence with both cameras simultaneously
   triggered
2. Compare frame counts and timestamps pair-by-pair, confirm no drift or dropped
   frames across a sustained capture run
3. Quantify residual sync error in milliseconds using the known drop speed as a
   ground-truth clock
4. Document and photograph the full modification and wiring for the review
   write-up

### Week 3: Stereo Calibration

**Phase 3A: Calibration**

1. Perform checkerboard stereo calibration for the camera pair at the intended
   working distance
2. Measure and record the physical baseline distance between cameras
3. Validate calibration accuracy against known reference distances placed in the
   scene

**Phase 3B: Static Depth Accuracy**

1. Measure stereo-derived depth for objects at several known distances (static,
   non-moving)
2. Record depth error (cm) at each distance, before introducing motion

### Week 4–5: Ballistic Trajectory Testing — Close Range

1. Set up the rig at close range (garden-scale, ~4m) with the 16mm lenses,
   confirming field of view suits the working distance
2. Run drop tests (1D) — predicted vs actual impact timing
3. Run lobbed/thrown tests (2D ballistic) — predicted vs actual landing position
4. Record accuracy across a minimum of 20 trials, varying speed and angle
5. Identify and document failure modes (motion blur at high speed, detection
   dropout at frame edges, lighting sensitivity)

### Week 6–7: Ballistic Trajectory Testing — Pitch Scale

1. Relocate the rig to a longer-range open space (neighbouring pitch) to test
   genuine flight-time trajectories
2. Re-calibrate for the new working distance if the lens/baseline setup changes
3. Run a minimum of 20 throw/kick trials at pitch scale, varying distance,
   speed, and angle
4. Compare pitch-scale accuracy directly against close-range results — this is
   the key comparative data point for the review, since most published stereo Pi
   camera examples are tabletop-scale only

### Week 8: Final Benchmarking & Write-up

**Phase 8A: Comprehensive Benchmarks**

| Benchmark | Method | Target |
|---|---|---|
| Sync accuracy | Timestamp comparison, dropped-ball reference | < 1 frame period offset |
| Frame rate achieved (synced) | PWM trigger frequency vs actual capture rate | Match within measurement tolerance |
| Static depth accuracy | Known-distance reference objects | Record error (cm) by distance |
| Close-range landing prediction error | 20 trials, garden-scale | Record error (cm) |
| Pitch-scale landing prediction error | 20 trials, pitch-scale | Record error (cm) |
| Time-to-land prediction error | Predicted vs actual | Record error (ms) |
| Modification reliability | Both units post-mod vs pre-mod capture quality | No degradation |

**Phase 8B: Review Documentation**

The final element14 community review will include:

- Full photographed hardware modification guide (R11 removal, XTR wiring) — the
  documentation gap this review specifically targets
- Trigger circuit schematic and working PWM sync code
- Stereo calibration and trajectory-fitting code (GitHub, public)
- All benchmark results with graphs comparing close-range vs pitch-scale
  accuracy
- Honest assessment of where the setup breaks down (fast/close motion, lighting,
  frame-edge dropout)
- Bill of materials with final costs and sourcing links
- Explicit notes on how this groundwork carries forward into a separate, larger
  interception-robot project

## 6. Risk Register

| Risk | Likelihood | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|
| SMD modification damages a camera board | Medium | Practice technique on a spare/scrap board first; proceed slowly with continuity testing at each step |
| XTR sync less reliable than documentation suggests | Medium | Quantify and report actual sync error rather than assuming perfection; fall back to software-timestamp sync with documented accuracy penalty if hardware sync proves unworkable |
| Global shutter camera stock/lead time delays receipt | Medium | Flagged and monitored at application time; substitute e-con Systems AR0234 module (same trigger-sync capability) if lead time slips materially |
| Outdoor pitch-scale testing affected by weather/lighting variability | Low | Multiple test sessions scheduled across the window; lighting conditions recorded per trial |
| CM5 dual-CSI throughput insufficient at target frame rate | Low | Benchmark early (Week 1); fall back to reduced resolution/frame rate if bandwidth-limited |

### Test Procedure

#### Week 1: Hardware Setup & Baseline

1. Bring up each SC0926 individually on the CM5's two CSI ports via libcamera;
   fit the 16mm lenses and confirm focus at the intended working distance.
2. Benchmark baseline single-camera frame rate and exposure range as shipped.
3. Remove R11 and solder a wire to the XTR touchpoint on each camera;
   continuity-test each modification before power-up.
4. Confirm each camera still captures correctly in normal (non-triggered) mode
   post-modification, ruling out accidental damage.

#### Week 2: Trigger Circuit & Synchronized Capture

1. Build a resistor-divider trigger circuit from a CM5 GPIO pin and wire both
   cameras' XTR lines to the shared trigger source.
2. Write a PWM trigger script; confirm pulse frequency directly controls the
   achieved frame rate on both cameras.
3. Capture a dropped-ball reference sequence with both cameras simultaneously
   triggered; compare frame counts and timestamps pair-by-pair for drift or
   dropped frames.
4. Quantify residual sync error in milliseconds against the known drop speed;
   photograph and document the full modification and wiring.

#### Week 3: Stereo Calibration

1. Perform checkerboard stereo calibration at the intended working distance;
   measure and record the physical baseline between cameras.
2. Validate calibration against known reference distances; measure static
   stereo depth error (cm) at several distances before introducing motion.

#### Week 4–5: Ballistic Trajectory Testing — Close Range

1. Set up the rig at garden scale (~4m); run drop tests (1D) and
   lobbed/thrown tests (2D ballistic).
2. Record accuracy across a minimum of 20 trials, varying speed and angle;
   document failure modes (motion blur, frame-edge detection dropout,
   lighting sensitivity).

#### Week 6–7: Ballistic Trajectory Testing — Pitch Scale

1. Relocate the rig to a longer-range open space, park;
   re-calibrate if the lens/baseline setup changes.
2. Run a minimum of 20 throw/kick trials, varying distance, speed, and angle.
3. Compare pitch-scale accuracy directly against close-range results — the key
   comparative data point, since most published stereo Pi camera examples are
   tabletop-scale only.

#### Week 8: Final Benchmarking & Write-up

1. Compile comprehensive benchmarks: sync accuracy, achieved frame rate,
   static depth accuracy, close- and pitch-scale landing-prediction error,
   time-to-land error, and modification reliability.
2. Publish the final review: photographed hardware modification guide,

   trajectory-fitting code (GitHub, public), benchmark graphs, an honest
   assessment of where the setup breaks down, bill of materials, and notes on
   how this groundwork carries into the later VENTUNO Q interception-robot
   project.
